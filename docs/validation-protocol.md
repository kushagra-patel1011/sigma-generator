# Validation protocol, round 1 (Windows)

This document fixes, before any measurement exists, how sigma-generator's rules are tested against executed
attacks and benign activity, and how the results will be read. It is committed before the sample is drawn: the
seed for the draw is the hash of the commit that adds this file, so the sample cannot be chosen, and the reading
of the results cannot be adjusted to fit them.

## 1. Question

The generator grades every rule **strong**, **moderate** or **weak** by what its detection block contains (see
`CLAUDE.md`, "Rule quality model"). Those grades have never been checked against attacks or real activity. Round 1
asks whether they mean what they claim: that a strong rule detects the technique it was written for, and alerts
less often on normal activity than a weak one.

## 3. Population and eligibility

**Population.** For every active technique in ATT&CK 19.2, the rule `generate` writes by default (the top-ranked
rule), where that rule's logsource product is `windows`. At this commit that is 322 rules: 76 strong, 123
moderate, 123 weak.

A rule is **eligible** when both hold:

1. its logsource is one the lab records (section 6): any Sysmon event category, `ps_script`, `ps_module`, or the
   Security, System or PowerShell channels (the list is `LAB_LOGSOURCES` in `tools/validation.py`); and
2. the pinned ART index has at least one test for **exactly** the rule's technique ID (a parent technique does not
   borrow its sub-techniques' tests, nor the reverse) that lists `windows` in `supported_platforms` and whose
   executor is not `manual`.

At this commit 203 rules are eligible: **63 of 76 strong, 80 of 123 moderate, 60 of 123 weak**. Every Windows
logsource in the population is recorded by the lab, so eligibility is decided by ART coverage alone.

**Eligibility is biased by tier.** 83% of strong rules are eligible against 49% of weak ones, because weak rules
concentrate on techniques ART does not exercise. The published results state this next to every number.

## 4. Sample

- **Size:** 50 rules, stratified by tier: **17 strong, 17 moderate, 16 weak**. Equal allocation, not
  proportional, so each tier gets an estimate of similar precision.
- **Seed:** the full hash of the commit that adds this file (`git log --diff-filter=A --format=%H --
  docs/validation-protocol.md`, the oldest entry).
- **Draw:** within each tier, eligible rules are sorted by `sha256("<seed>:<technique ID>")` as a hex string,
  ascending. The first 17 / 17 / 16 are the sample. The remainder, in the same order, is that tier's substitution
  queue.
- **Tool:** `python -m tools.validation draw` implements exactly this, and is committed together with this file.
  It refuses to draw if the generator has drifted from the corpus baseline. It writes `docs/validation/sample.json`,
  `sample.md`, and the 50 rule files under `docs/validation/rules/`. Those files are the rules under test.
- **Substitution:** a sampled rule is replaced by the next rule in its tier's queue **only** when none of its ART
  tests can be executed (every one fails its prerequisites, section 8). Every substitution is published with its
  reason. A rule that fails Sigma conversion is **not** substituted. It stays in the sample and counts as detecting
  nothing (section 8). The sample is never re-drawn.

## 5. Mapping rules to ART tests

A sampled rule is tested with **every** ART test that passes the eligibility filter for its technique, in the
index's order. No test is removed because it seems unlikely to trigger the rule's logsource: that judgement is
where selection bias would enter, and a narrow rule scoring low against its own technique's procedures is itself
a result. Tests are identified by `auto_generated_guid`, and the list for each rule is in `sample.json`.

At this commit the eligible rules carry a median of 2–3 tests each and a maximum of 92, so the per-rule detection
rate (section 11), not the pooled test count, is the primary measure.

## 6. Lab and telemetry

**Attack host.** One Windows 11 Enterprise VM (build recorded), standalone workgroup, local administrator,
Microsoft Defender real-time protection and tamper protection disabled so that tests are not blocked before they
produce telemetry. A clean snapshot is taken after installing Sysmon, the audit policy and the test runner.
Tests that need a domain, a second host or network services the VM does not have will fail their prerequisites,
and are reported as such (section 8).

**Benign hosts.** No attack test is ever run on these:

- **1 real endpoint:** the maintainer's daily-use Windows machine, ordinary work, at least 7 full UTC days.
  Defender stays on.
- **N lab VMs:** the same image as the attack host, but with Defender on, never used for attacks, running whatever
  activity is described in the results.

Results always publish these two separately, and describe the benign data as **"1 real endpoint + N lab VMs,
lower bound on false positives"**. Neither is presented as a fleet. The real endpoint is the one used for the
noise criterion (section 12).

**Telemetry on every host:** Sysmon with the pinned configuration, and the pinned audit policy and log sizes
(including process-creation command lines and PowerShell script-block logging). Logs are exported as `.evtx`.

**Host-days.** A benign host-day is one UTC calendar day for which the Sysmon, Security, System and PowerShell
logs are complete. That means the earliest retained event is before the day starts, and there is no log-clear event
(Security 1102, System 104) and no Sysmon service stop during the day. Incomplete days are excluded and
counted in the results.

**Clocks.** Every host synchronises to the same NTP source through w32time. `w32tm /query /status` is recorded at
the start and end of every test batch and every benign collection, and published. Detection windows (section 8)
are bounded by timestamps taken on the attack host itself, which is the same clock that stamps its events, so
skew between hosts cannot move an event in or out of a window. The NTP record exists so that the UTC day boundaries
used for false positives are trustworthy. A host whose recorded offset exceeds 2 s is flagged in the results.

## 7. Conversion

Each rule file (the 50 sampled rules, and every rule and correlation in the false-positive population of section
10) is converted with the pinned sigma-to-hayabusa-converter. The converter writes one Hayabusa rule per native log
source a Sigma logsource maps to: for example, `process_creation` becomes a Sysmon event 1 rule and a Security
4688 rule. Each converted rule's ID is derived from the original ID, and each keeps a `related: {type: derived}`
entry pointing back to it.

The **conversion manifest** maps each original rule ID to the set of converted rule IDs derived from it, read from
those `related` entries. It is published.

A rule the converter rejects, or converts into anything Hayabusa v4.1.0 refuses to load, is a **conversion
failure**. Conversion failures are published in their own table, per tier, with the converter's or Hayabusa's
message. They are a finding about the generated rules, not a skipped row: in the sample such a rule counts as
detecting nothing, and in the false-positive population it is left out of every noise denominator (a rule that
cannot run cannot alert, and counting it as quiet would flatter its tier) and reported next to it.

## 8. Execution and the definition of "detected"

For each sampled rule, for each of its ART tests, in order:

1. Restore the attack host's clean snapshot, boot, and wait 5 minutes for start-up activity to settle.
2. Run the test's prerequisite step (`Invoke-AtomicTest -GetPrereqs`). If it fails, record **prerequisites
   failed** with the runner's output, and move to the next test. The test is not executed and is excluded from the
   detection denominator.
3. Leave the host idle for a **quiet period** of 10 minutes, with no test activity.
4. Record `t0` (UTC, attack host), run the test, and record `t1` when the runner returns. If the runner reports
   that the test itself failed, record **execution failed**. The test stays in the denominator, since the attempt
   produced whatever telemetry it produced.
5. The **execution window** is `[t0 - 5 s, t1 + 60 s]`. Cleanup (`-Cleanup`) runs only after the window closes.
6. Export the host's logs.
7. The **null window** is cut afterwards from the quiet period. It has the same length `L` as the execution
   window, and ends where the execution window starts: `[t0 - 5 s - L, t0 - 5 s)`. If `L` is longer than the
   quiet period, the null window is the whole quiet period, and the test is flagged.

Hayabusa is run over each test's logs with only the converted rules of the rule under test loaded (`rules/config`
is the configuration shipped in the pinned release):

```
hayabusa dfir-timeline -d <test logs> -r <converted rules of this rule> -c rules/config -w -m informational \
  -n -D -u -U -p all-field-info -t jsonl -o alerts.jsonl
```

A test is **detected** by the rule under test when Hayabusa emits at least one alert that meets all three of:

- its `RuleID` is in the conversion-manifest set for the rule under test;
- its `Computer` is the attack host;
- its `Timestamp` (the event's own creation time, UTC) falls inside the execution window.

A test that would be detected, but where an alert meeting the first two conditions also falls inside the null
window, is **confounded**. The rule fires on the host's background activity, so the test cannot be credited.
Confounded tests count as **not detected** in every primary measure, and are reported separately.

Diagnostic only, not affecting any measure: a missed test is marked **telemetry absent** when the host recorded no
event at all of the rule's native log source (channel and event ID) inside the execution window. That separates
"the rule did not match" from "the configuration did not log it".

## 9. What is bucketed and published separately

- ART tests whose prerequisites failed (section 8, step 2), with the runner's output. They are not in any
  denominator.
- ART tests whose execution failed (section 8, step 4). They are in the denominator.
- Confounded tests (section 8).
- Rules that fail conversion (section 7). This is a finding, and the rule stays in its tier's results as detecting
  nothing.
- Substituted rules and their replacements (section 4).

## 10. False positives

**Population.** False positives are measured on every Windows rule in the section 3 population (322), not only on
the sample, because benign replay costs nothing extra per rule. That removes sampling error from the noise
question. Every Windows default correlation rule is included too: at this commit, 54 `temporal` chains, 28
`event_count` and 9 `value_count` rules, each counted where every step's logsource is Windows.

**Correlation rules are measured for false positives only. Chains are untested for detection in round 1**,
because an ART test runs one procedure, and a multi-step chain firing would need several at once. The results
say so wherever correlation numbers appear.

**Counting.** Hayabusa runs over each benign host's complete host-days with the whole converted population loaded,
using the same command as section 8. An alert is attributed to the original rule through the conversion manifest.
Alerts are then **deduplicated by (original rule, host, UTC day)**: one rule firing a thousand times on one host in
one day is one **alert-day**. The measure per rule is alert-days ÷ host-days, computed separately for the real
endpoint and for the lab VMs.

**Raw alert table.** Every alert is published, before deduplication: original rule ID, converted rule ID, host label,
UTC timestamp, channel, event ID. Every summary in the results can be recomputed from this table and the host-day
table. For the real endpoint, event contents (command lines, paths, user names) are not published. For the attack
host and lab VMs, the full Hayabusa output is published.

## 11. Measures published per tier

Detection (the 50-rule sample; the rule is the unit of analysis):

- rules drawn, substituted (with reasons) and evaluated; the eligible and population counts from section 3;
- ART tests mapped, executed, prerequisites failed, execution failed, confounded, telemetry absent;
- conversion failures;
- **per-rule detection rate**: detected tests ÷ executed tests for each rule (0 for a conversion failure); the
  tier's mean, with a 95% percentile bootstrap interval over rules (10,000 resamples, seeded with the protocol
  seed);
- share of rules detecting at least one test, with a Wilson 95% interval;
- pooled test-level detection rate, with a Wilson interval, as a secondary measure only, since tests cluster
  within rules.

False positives (the whole Windows population, real endpoint and lab VMs reported separately):

- conversion failures;
- share of rules with **zero alert-days**, with a Wilson 95% interval;
- alert-days per host-day across rules: median, 90th percentile, maximum;
- the same for correlation rules, by type.

The full per-rule table (technique, tier, tests executed, tests detected, confounded, alert-days per host and day)
is published alongside the summaries. No precision figure is published: without the rate of real attacks in the
benign data, it would mean nothing.

## 12. What would falsify the grading model

Decided now, before any result exists. The primary comparisons are strong against weak only. Moderate is reported
but is not used in any of these tests, so there are no extra comparisons to pick from.

- **Falsified on detection:** the weak tier's mean per-rule detection rate is **greater than or equal to** the
  strong tier's. Weak rules are broad, so a weak rule could score merely by matching the host's normal activity.
  The null window (section 8) is what stops that from counting as detection.
- **Falsified on noise:** on the real endpoint, the strong tier's share of rules with zero alert-days is **less than
  or equal to** the weak tier's: strong rules are as noisy as weak ones.
- **Supported:** strong is better than weak on both measures (a higher mean per-rule detection rate, and a higher
  share of rules with zero alert-days on the real endpoint), and the 95% interval of each difference excludes zero.
  That means a bootstrap over rules for detection, and a Newcombe hybrid-score interval for the zero-alert-day share.
- **Inconclusive:** anything else. It is reported as inconclusive, not as support.

If either falsification condition holds, it is **the headline finding**: the first sentence of the published
results, before any other number. It says the tiers are wrong in that respect.

## 13. Limitations stated in advance

- Windows only. Linux, macOS and cloud rules are not tested in round 1.
- Only rules whose technique has automated ART tests are eligible, and that favours strong rules (section 3).
- ART tests are published procedures run in isolation. They are not intrusions, and a rule detecting them says
  little about variants ART does not include.
- The benign data is one person's machine plus lab VMs: a lower bound on false positives, not an estimate for any
  organisation.
- Defender is disabled on the attack host, so detections there do not show what happens on protected hosts.
- One conversion path and one engine are used. A conversion failure may be the converter's limitation rather
  than the rule's, which is why the messages are published.
- With 16–17 rules per tier, intervals are wide (roughly ±20–25 points). A large difference between tiers can be
  shown; a small one cannot.
- Correlation rules are untested for detection.
